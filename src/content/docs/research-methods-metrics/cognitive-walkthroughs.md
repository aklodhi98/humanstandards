---
title: Cognitive Walkthroughs
description: Evaluate step-by-step whether a new user can achieve goals.
---

A cognitive walkthrough is a usability inspection method where you step through a task as a first-time user would, asking whether they'd know what to do at each point. It's cheap, fast, and doesn't require recruiting participants — just a realistic scenario and a critical eye.

## When to use it

Cognitive walkthroughs shine when you need to:

- Evaluate new user onboarding before launch
- Check whether a redesign makes sense to newcomers
- Find learnability problems without running full user tests
- Review a competitor's flow to understand their UX

They're less useful for expert users or tasks that rely on learned shortcuts.

## How to run one

### 1. Define the scenario

Start with a realistic user goal: "I want to sign up for a free trial" or "I need to cancel my subscription." Be specific about who the user is — their background, motivation, and what they already know.

### 2. List the ideal action sequence

Write down every step a user must take to complete the task. Be granular: include clicking, scrolling, reading, and any decision points. This becomes your walkthrough script.

### 3. Walk through each step

At each action, ask four questions:

- **Will they try to achieve this effect?** Does the user's goal align with this step?
- **Will they see the control?** Is the button, link, or field visible and noticeable?
- **Will they recognize it?** Does the label or icon communicate what it does?
- **Will they understand the feedback?** After acting, will they know if it worked?

If you answer "no" or "maybe" to any question, you've found a potential problem.

### 4. Document issues and fixes

Log each breakdown: where it occurred, why, and what might fix it. Prioritize by severity and frequency across tasks.

### 5. Iterate

Fix the issues and walk through again. Repeat until the flow makes sense.

## Tips for better walkthroughs

- Include multiple evaluators to catch blind spots
- Be genuinely skeptical — pretend you've never seen the product
- Don't assume users will read instructions or help text
- Focus on the first-time experience, not power-user shortcuts
