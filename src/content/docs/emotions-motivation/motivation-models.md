Why do people do what they do? And more importantly, how can we design experiences that encourage action without manipulation? Understanding motivation helps you build products that genuinely serve users — not exploit them.

## The Fogg Behavior Model

BJ Fogg's model is beautifully simple: behavior happens when motivation, ability, and a prompt come together at the same moment. Miss any one of these, and nothing happens.

- **Motivation** is why someone wants to act. It's driven by seeking pleasure, avoiding pain, hoping for good outcomes, or wanting social acceptance.
- **Ability** is whether they *can* act. Time, money, effort, mental load, and how far it deviates from their routine all factor in.
- **Prompt** is the trigger — the notification, button, or reminder that says "do this now." It has to be noticeable and appear at the right moment.

The key insight: if motivation is low, make the action easier. If the action is hard, boost motivation. And always prompt at the right time.

## Self-Determination Theory

For motivation that lasts, look to intrinsic drivers. Self-Determination Theory identifies three psychological needs:

- **Autonomy**: People want to feel in control. Give them meaningful choices, not just the illusion of options.
- **Competence**: People want to feel capable. Provide clear feedback, achievable challenges, and visible progress.
- **Relatedness**: People want connection. Support social features, shared goals, and community without forcing them.

Products that support these needs create genuine engagement. Those that ignore them rely on tricks that eventually backfire.

## Use these powers for good

- Reduce friction to make things easier — not to remove safeguards.
- Time prompts to genuine user intent, not manufactured urgency.
- Avoid dark patterns: fake scarcity, guilt trips, social pressure, and confusing opt-outs.
- Let users control gamification and social features.
- Design for long-term trust, not short-term conversion bumps.

## Common mistakes

- **Over-relying on badges and points**: Extrinsic rewards can actually undermine intrinsic motivation over time.
- **All prompt, no ability**: Triggering action before users are ready leads to frustration and abandonment.
- **Missing the moment**: High motivation + easy task + no prompt = a missed opportunity.
- **Manipulation over value**: Coercive prompts might work once, but they destroy trust.
